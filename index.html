About

I work at the intersection of economics, actuarial science, the mathematics of finance, and data analytics, combining theoretical rigor with experience in the financial services industry.

My PhD thesis, Essays in Applied Econometrics, applies three different econometric approaches to three problems: panel data methods to the incidence of US state corporate taxes on wages, causal machine learning to the effect of financial inclusion on resilience, and financial econometrics to whether volatility-control indices do what their name claims.

My fields are financial economics and econometrics, public economics and policy, macroeconomics, and computational and quantitative economics.

Research

Working papers — economics and finance

Do US state corporate taxes have heterogeneous effects on county-level wages?

Corporate tax changes appear to leave wages untouched when increases and decreases are forced to act symmetrically. Allowed to differ, a one point rise in the top marginal rate cuts service-sector wages by 1.08 percent, while a one point cut raises them by 0.44 percent.

Abstract

This paper exploits US state corporate taxes in a setting of contiguous counties straddling state borders to estimate the incidence of corporate taxes on wages. The analysis constructs a sample that captures all possible tax variations, including tax increases, decreases, and no changes between neighboring counties. The study examines the heterogeneous effect of changes in US state corporate taxes on county wages at the industry level.

Results reveal that wages are not significantly affected by tax changes when the effects are assumed symmetric. However, when treated asymmetrically, tax increases and decreases have a significant economic and statistical impact on county wages in the service-producing industry. A percentage point increase in the top marginal corporate tax rate leads to a 1.08 percent decrease in wages while a percentage point decrease leads to a 0.44 percent increase in wages. There are no significant effects in the goods-producing market.

Does financial inclusion promote financial resilience?

Account ownership raises financial resilience by 7.2 percent across Sub-Saharan Africa, and the gains run largest in low-income economies rather than middle-income ones — a pattern that pre-defined subgroup analysis tends to miss.

Abstract

This paper examines how account ownership impacts financial resilience across different population segments in Sub-Saharan Africa. Traditional methods for estimating heterogeneous treatment effects often segment samples into pre-defined subgroups, potentially missing significant variations across populations. To overcome these limitations, I apply causal machine learning techniques that combine causal inference in a machine learning framework to estimate the conditional average treatment effects (CATE) of account ownership.

Leveraging data from the Global Findex of the World Bank, I employ honest causal trees and a two-stage validation process, combining cluster analysis and super learner algorithms, to provide robust, nuanced insights. Results indicate that account ownership increases financial resilience by 7.2% in Sub-Saharan Africa, with more pronounced effects in low-income SSA economies (9.4%) compared to middle-income SSA economies (8.9%). These insights underscore the importance of targeted financial inclusion policies for economically vulnerable populations in the region.

Do volatility control funds really control volatility?

Both the risk control and managed risk strategies do control volatility, but the managed risk index stabilizes it better — a distinction between controlling volatility and managing it that matters for institutional portfolios.

Abstract

This study provides an empirical analysis of volatility control strategies by comparing S&P 500 risk control (SPXT18UT) and S&P 500 managed risk (SPXMR) indices with their underlying basis, the S&P 500 total return (SPXTR) index. Using rolling window, MIDAS, and GARCH-type models, the study estimates conditional volatility and assesses how these strategies perform across different volatility regimes, defined by the VIX index.

The results show that both strategies effectively control volatility, with the managed risk strategy (SPXMR) demonstrating superior volatility stabilization. By distinguishing between volatility control and volatility management, this research offers valuable insights into the performance of these strategies, highlighting their importance for institutional investors in managing portfolio risk. The findings contribute to the academic literature and practice by offering a clear framework for understanding volatility control strategies and their effectiveness in real-world market conditions.

Working papers — mathematics and statistics of finance

Univariate extreme value analysis of quantitative investment strategies

Volatility-controlled strategies handle tail risk better than the conventional equity indices beneath them, and the managed risk strategy shows stronger extreme value characteristics than risk control — a gap the literature on variable annuity portfolios had left open.

Abstract

The evolution of product development in the variable annuity (VA) market has generated growing interest in quantitative investment management, particularly as many VA issuers have incorporated volatility-controlled funds into their annuity portfolios. While empirical research on extreme value analysis in traditional investments exists, there is a significant gap in the literature regarding risk modeling in volatility-controlled funds.

This study addresses this gap by analyzing and modeling the extreme values of investments in volatility-controlled funds and comparing them with conventional equity funds. Specifically, the financial returns of the S&P Dow Jones Indices — including the SPXTR, risk control SPXT18UT, and managed risk SPXMR indices — are examined using extreme value theory (EVT) methods, such as block maxima and peaks over threshold.

The results show that the managed risk strategy demonstrates superior extreme value characteristics when compared to the corresponding risk control strategy. Furthermore, volatility-controlled strategies outperform their underlying conventional equity indices in terms of managing extreme risks.

Working papers — actuarial science

Analyzing portfolio-specific mortality experience

Fitting actuarial mortality laws directly to pension portfolio experience produces smooth rates without graduation, and fits observed mortality substantially better at post-retirement ages.

Abstract

Mortality and longevity have a profound financial impact on the insurance and pension industries. As a result, accurately modeling mortality rates is critical for pricing, reserving, and managing the risks associated with discrepancies between expected and actual mortality outcomes.

Traditional actuarial techniques for mortality analysis are increasingly being replaced by statistical models, particularly survival models, which track mortality continuously at the individual level. This paper explores the modeling and analysis of mortality within a pension portfolio by fitting actuarial mortality laws to observed mortality experience data.

Actuarial mortality laws inherently exhibit smoothness and therefore do not require graduation. Assuming a specific mathematical form for the hazard function — equivalently, assuming a continuous distribution for an individual's lifetime — leads to smooth fitted mortality rates. The analysis shows that the actuarial laws provide substantially better fits at post-retirement ages.
